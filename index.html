The Ebbinghaus Problem

Your memory is leaking right now

Hermann Ebbinghaus discovered in 1885 that we forget exponentially. Without active recall, 80% of new material vanishes within 24 hours. Rereading your notes creates an illusion of knowing — you feel familiar, not fluent.

Every Synapse lab forces retrieval: you produce the answer from memory before seeing it. That act of effortful recall — even when you get it wrong — rewires the neural pathway. Psychologists call it the testing effect.

When you flag uncertainty ("I guessed"), the system returns that item sooner. Items you know cold are spaced out. This mirrors how memory consolidation actually works during sleep.

Cognitive Science

Four principles powering every drill

Every interaction in Synapse is engineered around peer-reviewed findings in cognitive psychology and memory neuroscience.

🧠
Principle 01
Retrieval Practice (Testing Effect)

Generating an answer from memory — even unsuccessfully — produces stronger recall than rereading. The act of retrieval itself strengthens the memory trace. This is why Synapse never shows you the answer first.

Roediger & Karpicke, Science 2006 · Kornell & Son, 2009
📅
Principle 02
Spaced Repetition (SRS)

Items you know well are shown less often. Items you struggle with return sooner. Synapse tracks each item individually — if you guessed, it comes back in 3 cards; if you knew it cold, it returns next session.

Ebbinghaus 1885 · Cepeda et al., Psych. Bulletin 2006
⚡
Principle 03
Desirable Difficulties

Learning that feels harder produces better retention. Synapse interleaves question types (fill-the-blank, build-the-line, contrast pairs) rather than blocking them. The confusion is the point — it forces deeper processing.

Bjork & Bjork, 1992 · Kornell & Bjork, 2008
🎯
Principle 04
Confidence Calibration

After each answer, Synapse asks: did you know it, or did you guess? This metacognitive check — rating your own certainty — is itself a retrieval cue that improves future recall by 30%. Overclaiming is the hidden enemy.

Dunlosky et al., Psych. Science Public Interest 2013
